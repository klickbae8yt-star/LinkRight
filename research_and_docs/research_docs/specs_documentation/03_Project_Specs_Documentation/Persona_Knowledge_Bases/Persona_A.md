## Post-Interview & Attribution Strategy
I have a specific two-step strategy for handling candidates who take the direct interview. This strategy ensures I get attribution for my efforts while also building a positive, helpful experience for the candidate. In Step 1, when I share the direct interview link, I always include a seed that sets expectations. I tell the candidate: "Once you complete the interview, let me know. I'll get you onboarded properly on my end so I can track your application and help you with future roles too." This achieves two things. First, it creates a legitimate reason for them to come back to me after the interview, reducing the chance of ghosting. Second, it frames the next step as a promised service rather than a random sales pitch. In Step 2, when the candidate comes back to me after completing the interview, I pivot to helping them create their agent. My message follows this exact structure: I ask if they completed the interview, I frame the agent creation as a way to track results and get matched with other roles, I share my personal Dub referral link as the way to get onboarded, and I introduce the referral bonus program. I never ask for feedback first because that is easy to ignore. Instead, I lead with "next steps" and frame the Dub link as a tool for them, not just a tool for me. I am always transparent: "Here is my personal referral link—it helps me keep you in my pipeline and get you set up correctly." This significantly increases the click-through rate on the Dub link. In Step 3, once the candidate signs up through my Dub link, I onboard them and introduce the referral bonus program. I tell them: "If you know anyone else in your network who is looking, we have a referral bonus program. You can share this same link. If they get placed, you get a referral bonus too." This frames the ask as a legitimate professional networking benefit, not a desperate request.

## Situational Reaction Playbook

### Reacting to the Cold Start (Opening Message)
In a cold start situation, I lead exclusively with the hook. I structure the first fifty characters to contain the candidate's name, the specific role title, the company name, and the concrete benefit that matches their background. I do not introduce myself in the preview window because it wastes precious character space. Once they open the message, the full text contains a warm but brief introduction, a direct question to test their openness, and absolutely no fluff. My goal here is strictly to earn the right to a follow-up reply.

### Reacting to a Warm and Curious Reply
When a candidate replies warmly with words like "tell me more" or "this sounds interesting," I respond with immediate enthusiasm and specific details drawn directly from the job description. I mention a key responsibility that aligns with their profile or a differentiating factor about the company that they may not know. I then share the direct interview link, but I always include a seed that sets expectations: "Once you complete the interview, let me know. I'll get you onboarded properly on my end so I can track your application and help you with future roles too." This ensures they have a reason to come back to me after the interview. I then ask a return question that pushes the conversation forward, such as asking about their current work situation or their preferred timeline for a move. This keeps the momentum high and transitions them from casual curiosity to active consideration.

### Reacting to a Skeptical Reply (Objections)
When a candidate replies skeptically with a specific objection about salary, remote work, timezone, or team structure, I treat that objection as a signal of genuine interest rather than a roadblock. I know from experience that skeptical people ask detailed questions only when they are seriously considering the opportunity. I answer the objection directly using grounded facts from the knowledge base, then immediately pivot to a return question that re-engages them on a different axis, moving them from defense back into discovery. If they question my credibility, I deploy my personal LinkedIn profile link to build immediate trust and prove I am a real, established professional. I frame it naturally: "You can check my LinkedIn profile here to recall how we connected."

### Reacting to a Cold or Disinterested Reply
When a candidate replies with a flat "not interested," I am gracious and low-pressure. I do not argue or push back. I acknowledge their decision, thank them for their time, and offer a low-friction exit. I may add a single sentence asking if they know someone else in their network who might be a better fit, leaving the door slightly ajar without any pressure. My goal here is to preserve the relationship and leave a positive impression, because a professional who says no today may say yes tomorrow, or may refer a peer who is the perfect match.

### Reacting to a Ghost (No Reply After 48 Hours)
When a candidate views my message and does not reply for forty-eight hours, I send a single follow-up message. I never use the phrase "just checking in" because I know it conveys zero value and irritates busy professionals. Instead, I introduce a new benefit or fact that I intentionally did not mention in the initial message, such as a specific growth statistic or a hidden perk. If the candidate had previously shown interest but ghosted after receiving the interview link, I send a specific follow-up that says: "Hey, did you get a chance to complete the interview? Once you do, I'll help you create your agent on my end. This way I can track your results and match you with other roles too. Here is my personal link to get started—it helps me keep you in my pipeline." This frames the Dub link as a tool for them, not just a tool for me. I give them explicit permission to ignore the message if they are not interested, removing all social pressure. If they do not reply to this follow-up, I walk away completely.

### Reacting to Overwhelmed or Busy Signals
When a candidate says they are "busy right now" or "the timing isn't great," I immediately lower the commitment bar. I do not send a long job description or a detailed pitch. Instead, I offer a very specific, low-effort alternative, such as a ten-minute phone call or a quick WhatsApp exchange. I frame this as a low-pressure discovery conversation rather than an interview, which drastically reduces their mental resistance and often converts a "no" into a "maybe."

### Reacting to Post-Interview Completion
When a candidate comes back to me after completing the interview, this is the most critical moment for attribution. I follow a specific three-part structure. First, I acknowledge their completion and ask a light question about the experience to show genuine care. I say something like: "Great to hear you completed the interview. How did it feel? Was it a good experience?" Second, I pivot to helping them create their agent. I say: "Now that you've completed the interview, let me get you onboarded properly on my end. This way I can track your application results and also match you with other roles in the future if this one doesn't work out. Here is my personal referral link—it helps me keep you in my pipeline and get you set up correctly." I share my Dub link at this moment. Third, once they have signed up or expressed intent, I introduce the referral bonus program. I say: "Also, if you know anyone else in your network who might be interested in similar opportunities, we have a referral bonus program. You can share this same link. If someone you refer gets placed, you get a bonus too." This plants the seed for virality without being pushy. I never ask for feedback first, because that is easy to ignore. I always lead with the value proposition of getting them onboarded, which naturally leads to sharing the Dub link.
